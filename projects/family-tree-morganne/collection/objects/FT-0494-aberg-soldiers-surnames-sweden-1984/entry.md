## Catalog note

**Historian narrative (not a family muster).** Åberg explains how Swedish soldiers received a **military surname** in addition to baptismal name + patronymic, and how that surname **stayed with the rote** so the next occupant inherited it even when unrelated. That mechanism can *look* like a name hand-off in later family memory. **It does not prove this Rudd line swapped Anderson for Rudd.**

## Excerpts

### Patronymic vs military surname

> It was only with the advent of **indelningsverket** some 300 years ago that the soldiers in other provinces began to assume other surnames rather than the ancient **patronymic or -son name**.

> In an official proclamation, signed by **Charles XI in 1690**, it was stated that regular muster rolls were to be established for every regiment and that these rolls were to record the soldier's baptismal name, **his patronymic as well as his military surname**.

### The next man on the croft inherited the name

> Each infantry regiment had 150 rotar or military districts and each rote was named for the first soldier assigned to that district. **His name was then inherited by the soldiers, which followed him, even though the former occupant and the present occupant were not related.**

> If the soldier moved from one rote to another, which happened occasionally, **he took the name from the new rote**.

### Some soldier names became family names

> A surprisingly large number of soldiers' names remained alive and were **carried on by the heirs** of the soldier or cavalryman.

## Supports

- Supports: Independent War-Archives historian: military surnames were official; they replaced (for service) the -son patronymic; they often passed to the *next soldier on the same rote*, not by blood; many later became hereditary family names
- Does not support: This family’s Anderson origin; two men trading names to avoid the army; a named regiment or rote for Claus’s father
