## What “tailor” usually meant for Russia-born Jewish men ~1900

Gotham Center’s Garment Industry History Project:

> America’s garment industry exploded after the Civil War, and no place more than in New York City, where newly arrived immigrants, especially Jews, used the **sewing and tailoring skills** they acquired in the “Old Country” to earn a living. They began at home, doing the work themselves, then hiring a few workers, then forming small enterprises…

Garment District Alliance history (*A Stitch in Time*):

> …the garment industry during that period employed roughly **half of all the city’s Jewish males**… At the end of the 19th century, garment production was done **at home**… The apparel trade was centered in the **Lower East Side**…

LOC classroom materials on Eastern European Jewish immigrants summarize the labor reality many faced:

> More than one-half of all Eastern European Jewish immigrants worked in manual occupations, predominantly in the garment industry… countless tiny, airless sweatshop factories…

**Say with family:** “Tailor” on a Russia-born father’s NYC birth return is usually **needle-trade livelihood** in that boom — home shop, contractor shop, or loft — not a bespoke Savile Row image. We do **not** know which tier Solomon sat in.

## Why West 35th matters (geography, with a timing caveat)

The later formal **Garment District** settled roughly **34th–42nd Streets between Broadway and Ninth** (the old Tenderloin). *A Stitch in Time:*

> The area finally settled upon was between 9th Avenue and Broadway and bounded by **34th Street and 42nd Street**.

**68 W 35th** sits on the **south edge** of that corridor. In **1902** the big midtown loft “Garment Center” build-out was still ahead (peak construction 1919–30s); manufacturers were already pushing north from the Lower East Side toward midtown department stores (e.g. B. Altman at Fifth & 34th, 1906). So the address is **on the map of the coming garment midtown** — it does **not** prove Solomon worked in a 1925 Seventh Avenue loft.
