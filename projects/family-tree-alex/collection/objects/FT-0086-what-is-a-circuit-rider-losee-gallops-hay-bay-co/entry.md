## What a circuit rider was (one paragraph)

A **circuit rider** (also “saddlebag preacher” or “traveling preacher”) was a Methodist minister assigned to a **circuit** — a loop of settlements with no settled pastor. He rode horseback (or walked, or crossed ice/water) from cabin to cabin and meeting-house to meeting-house, carrying little more than what fit in saddlebags, preaching almost daily, sleeping wherever a family would take him, then riding on. Covering a long circuit could take **weeks**. The job was travel + preaching + organizing “classes,” not sitting in one church.

## Story 1 — William Losee: gallop, ice, and “Smite him”

Losee was among the first Methodist circuit riders regularly appointed to Upper Canada. Carroll’s portrait:

> Losee has been described as being at the period at which we write, about twenty-eight years of age, rather tall and active,—and, despite a **shrivelled arm**, an agile and fearless horseman, **usually riding upon the gallop**. As a preacher he was more hortatory than expository. He was impassioned, voluble, fearless, and denunciatory… praying God to “smite sinners!”

Returning for his second season, he **crossed the ice as soon as it would bear his horse**, having first walked the Black River forests without the horse:

> …entering the Province… by **crossing the ice so soon as it would bear his horse**, which he had not brought with him in his first journey,—having traversed the forests of the Black River country…

When a man mocked him in a meeting, Carroll records Losee’s prayer and the sudden collapse (early convert tradition names **Joseph Brouse** among that region’s converts):

> …in answer to Losee’s prayer, who, on seeing his misconduct, lifted his eyes and hands to heaven and cried out, “**Smite him, my God! My God, smite him!**” He fell like a bullock under the stroke of the butcher’s axe, and writhed on the floor in agony, until the Lord in mercy set his soul at liberty. Other early converts in that region were, Michael Carman, Peter Brouse, and John Van-Camp.

**Reading note for kids:** Tell it as history people *wrote down* about a dramatic conversion — not as a magic trick to copy. The point is how intense and personal frontier preaching felt.

## Story 2 — Darius Dunham: axe at dawn, then a joke

Dunham (the man Father Shorts later credited with his conversion) lodged overnight in a home. The husband of a woman Dunham’s preaching had reached came before dawn **with an axe**:

> The husband came to the house one morning where Dunham had lodged over night, and inquired for him before he was up. Being hastily summoned, the preacher made his appearance only partially dressed, when the infuriated man made towards him **armed with an axe**, and would have slain him had it not been for the prompt and vigorous intervention of the man and woman of the house, who succeeded in disarming the assailant. Dunham’s calmness and Christian fidelity… brought the man to reason, and penitence, and prayer at once, and issued in his conversion… his house became “a lodging place for way-faring men.”  
> This narrative the writer had from Mr. Jacob Peterson, a son-in-law of Mr. Dunham.

And the famous wit (why circuit riders rode good horses):

> A new-made “Squire,” rallied Dunham… about riding so fine a horse, and told him he was very unlike his humble Master, who was content to ride on an ass. The preacher responded… that he would most assuredly imitate his Master in that particular but for the difficulty of finding the animal required—the “**government having made up all the asses into magistrates!**”
