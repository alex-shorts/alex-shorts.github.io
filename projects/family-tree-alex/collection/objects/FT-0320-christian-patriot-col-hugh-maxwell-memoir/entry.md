## Catalog note

Daughter’s 1833 memoir of **Col. Hugh Maxwell** (b. Minterburn **27 Apr 1733** O.S.). 2026-08-09 mint: two Hughs, Fort William Henry escape, Bunker Hill wound. **2026-08-12 expansion** (same URL, no remint): surveying; m. **Bridget Monroe** of Lexington (FAN); Heath cabin / liberty school; home-front winters; Long Island boat letter; Saratoga / Valley Forge / Monmouth; Stickney pay letters (fortune-hunters); pension refused; death at sea **14 Oct 1799**.

## Full text

### Identity — two Hughs + Benjamin sibling

> Hugh Maxwell was born, April 27, O. S. 1733, in the town of Minterburn, county Tyrone, Ireland. His father of the same name, being a Calvinist, and disliking the established church of Ireland, determined upon removing to America… the subject of this sketch being only six weeks old… Hugh settled on a hired farm in Bedford, Mass. … In 1759, my grand-father, was found dead in the road, supposed to have been killed by falling from his horse. He had four children after he came to this country. Most of his children lived to a great age. William, the eldest, died at 95, Margaret 99, Hugh 67, Sarah upwards of 90, Benjamin 92, James 83, Thompson 93.

### French and Indian — Fort William Henry (1757)

> …laid seige to Fort William Henry, where my father was then stationed… My father was among the prisoners, and the Indians caught him, and stripped him of every article except his pantaloons, when he slipped from their hands, seized a gun, and ran for life towards Fort Edward… At length, on reaching what was called Half-way Brook, he stopped to slake his thirst; but on looking back, he saw two Indians holding a white man whom they were just ready to tomahawk… he therefore again ran for the fort, where he arrived in safety.

### Lexington / Minute-Men / Bunker Hill wound

> In the spring of 1755 [sic — context is 1775], my father was appointed lieutenant of a company of minute-men… On the 19th… British soldiers marched from Boston, through Lexington to Concord… My father… marched with his company to Cambridge, and joined Col. Prescott's regiment.

> During the action on the hill, my father was wounded, by a ball passing through his right shoulder. It entered just under the collar bone and came out through the shoulder blade… though his arm dropped and hung useless by his side, he proceeded through a shower of shot and bullets… He was carried to Cambridge, and placed under the care of Dr. Hart… A gangrene commenced, but… he recovered; although he was never afterwards able to do the work of a farmer.

> On the 5th of July he wrote as follows: "I am yet very lame with my shoulder… He took out one splinter of bone yesterday, two to day… Nine pieces of bone were extracted from the wound, some of them an inch in length."

### Surveying, Bridget Monroe, Heath cabin, liberty school (expanded 2026-08-12)

> The family had no advantages of a school when young. The mother, with no books except her Bible and some other religious books, taught all her seven children… My father alone went to school a short time, to learn surveying, an art which he practised with success in after life. … In one of his journals he says, "My parents early taught me the principles of liberty and religion, which have supported me through many difficulties and hardships."

> On returning home at the close of the campaign of 1759, he married Miss Bridget Monroe, of Lexington. They had seven children, all of whom, excepting the eldest, are still living.

> In the year 1770, he gratefully records a peculiarly striking display of God's power and goodness. His house was struck by lightning, and one end of it shattered to pieces. Eight persons were in the room… yet no one was hurt excepting one child. She was, for a time, thought to be dead, but soon recovered, although her arm and side were badly scorched.

> In 1773 he removed his family to Charlemont… it is now Heath… a small house of one room and a closet. … often, in the winter's morning, would they find themselves covered some inches deep with snow, that had drifted through the chinks in the roof.

> The two succeeding winters after he removed, he kept a small school; and there too he was careful to instil into the minds of the young men the love of liberty, and a determination to resist oppression… The consequence was, that in 1775, it appeared that every man in that and the neighboring towns, was in favor of liberty.

### Home front while he was at war

> …no one to do it but his wife and children. The eldest daughter was but fourteen, and the eldest son but six. … Twice a day some one must go to the barn to keep the cattle from perishing. … Many times they had to go on their hands and knees over the high snow drifts… Then would they say, "Ah, this dreadful war!"

> Late in the fall, he left home again… against the remonstrance and tears of his wife… But he considered the call of his distressed country paramount to every other.

### Long Island / Governor's Island letter, 5 Sept 1776

> "New-York, Sept. 5, 1776. My Dear: … Last Friday night, General Washington thought proper to withdraw all the force from Long Island… He told us we were all prisoners; and really, I did believe we were… So that I did not think I should see you till I had seen Old England."

> "But behold the power of Heaven displayed! General Washington sent us seven boats… I came with the last party through their fire. We had not a man killed, and but two wounded… Is there not a superior Power, that could protect us in open boats… through such a shower of shot?"

### Saratoga, Valley Forge, Monmouth (his presence)

> My father was in these actions, and with the suffering army at Morristown during the winter. In the spring of 1777, Gen. Washington detached the brigade to which my father belonged, to join the northern army under Gen. Gates… On the 7th of Oct. another battle took place, concerning which my father writes as follows: "Through the goodness of God to me, I am safe and well… The day before yesterday, our brigade had another sore battle with the enemy. … With nothing but small arms, we forced the British from eight cannon…"

> In November, my father joined the southern army, near Philadelphia, and shared its hardships when, in the latter part of December, they sat down in the roads near Valley Forge, to build huts… many men destitute of shoes and stockings, and marking their footsteps over the frozen ground, with blood from their lacerated feet.

> The next action in which he was engaged, was the battle of Monmouth, N. J. on the 23th [sic — 28th] of June, 1778. … In the year 1779, my father was promoted to the rank of Major, and placed under command of Major General William Heath…

### Rank close, Heath friendship, Washington in the yard (expanded 2026-08-12)

Ensign (French war): *His commission as Ensign was dated March 31, 1759, which was his rank at the close of the war.*

Heath, 13 Feb 1799 — the documented friendship (not Washington):

> Roxbury, February 13, 1799. Dear Sir — … I well know your long and faithful services in the army, and how often I have slept without apprehension of being surprised, because you guarded the out-post, and I knew that the enemy would not be allowed to evade your vigilance. … The sixth [copy of my Memoirs] I present to you, in token of my particular friendship. … W. HEATH.

> …when the town of Charlemont was divided… my father used his influence and had the new town named after so meritorious a revolutionary patriot.

Heath order, 22 Dec 1780: a detachment “commanded by Major Maxwell, an exceeding attentive and good officer, whose whole conduct has had my fullest approbation.”

Washington, 26–27 Mar 1783 — group greeting, not a private letter. Maxwell to his wife:

> Yesterday after the court had met and were standing without on the steps of the door, general Washington came riding up, and after a “good morrow to ye gentlemen,” “a fine day general,” and about one or two minutes silence, the general took off his hat and addressed us in nearly the following words: “Gentlemen, I think I may now venture to congratulate you on the establishment of peace. Last night an express arrived from congress with the account of it…”

Lt. Col. commission (daughter’s dating):

> In Aug. 1783, before leaving the army, my father received a commission as Lieut. Colonel, in the Massachusetts line, to take rank October 12, 1782, which was his rank at the close of the war.

The June 1780 “letter from Gen. Washington” printed in this book is Ramsay’s extract of Washington describing the army’s hunger — **not** a letter addressed to Maxwell. Continental dates → **FT-0434** (Heitman). Founders Online names his company in a letter *to* Washington → same object.

### Stickney letters — pay the army (expanded 2026-08-12)

Daughter: he wrote two letters to friend **William Stickney, Esq.** of Massachusetts on currency and army pay while the army waited out the peace. The fortune-hunters appeal (memoir pp. 106–107):

> Sir, — It is currently reported in camp that we are on the eve of a peace. … Now, sir, suppose the end accomplished, peace, liberty, and independence, soon, suffer me to ask what you will do with your army? Many will say disband it at once. I say so too. But sir, will you turn us into the wide world to seek our fortunes like fortune-hunters, without money or friends, after we have served you eight years in your wars? after spending the flower of our days in the service of our country…

Earlier in the same letter-thread he wants only depreciation made good — not half-pay — and to “return back among my old neighbors and be a citizen, not a soldier.” STCC paraphrase → **FT-0321**; primary here.

### Genesee survey pay, pension refused, death at sea

> At the close of his services, he was offered in payment, one of the most eligibly situated townships of land on the tract. But the necessities of his family required that he should take his pay in money, for present use.

> …on the 29th of December, 1794, he left home and repaired to Philadelphia… "Monday, January 12, 1795. — Waited on the Secretary of War…" … "by which law I am deprived of eleven years compensation for the disability which I have suffered." Thus ended his prospect of obtaining relief from a pension.

> He purchased some shipping horses and with them embarked on board a vessel at Hartford, for the West Indies. He left home in July, 1799, and in October we heard the news of his death. … three days before the vessel arrived in port, homeward bound, he was taken with a fever. … He died October 14th, 1799, in the 67th year of his age.
