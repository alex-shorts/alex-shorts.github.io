## Catalog note

**Material culture for the prairie-farmer story.** John × Sophia Gatz and son Charles farmed in **McPherson County, Kansas**. We do **not** have a photo of their house. These open photographs show what Plains settlers’ **housing and field work** looked like in the same era — so the profession comes alive without inventing family biography.

## Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Sod house / “soddy”** | Walls of stacked prairie sod (grass + root mat); used when timber was scarce |
| **Dugout** | Shelter cut into a hillside — cheaper first shelter |
| **Frame farmhouse** | Lumber walls once rail + cash allowed — the upgrade path many families took |
| **Breaking sod** | First plow pass cutting virgin prairie into crop land — hard draft-animal work |
| **Horse-drawn plow** | Primary tillage tool before widespread tractor power |

Solomon Butcher (*Sod Houses…*, 1904) on the pioneer’s job:

> …has to devise the ways and means for taking the country as nature has left it… and bring it into subjection to the will of man…

## Was it “Little House on the Prairie”?

**Mood overlap only — not the same status.**

| | Ingalls (*Little House*, SE Kansas ~1869–70) | Gatz / Hilgenfeld McPherson (1880s–1900s) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Land | Brief squatters on Osage Reserve | Legal Midwest settlers; Charles on Pioneer Register **1889** |
| Stay | ~1 year, then left | Permanent farm generation (stones 1905–06) |
| County stage | Contested frontier | Organizing wheat county with rail by ~1879–80 |

So: same **prairie hardship vocabulary** (sod, plow, wind, isolation in photos from neighboring Plains counties) — different **legal and economic footing**. Our Altmark Germans arrived via the Hilgenfeld Midwest path ([FT-0097](../FT-0097/entry.md)), not as Osage-Reserve squatters.

**Also:** McPherson had large Mennonite / Russian-German colonies — ambient neighbors, **not** proven identity for this Gatz line.
