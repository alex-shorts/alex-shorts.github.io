## Catalog note

**Profession story.** Monroe’s 1921 death certificate says **“Jobber, shoe business.”** That word meant something specific in his generation. This Accession unpacks the trade so the family can read what the job *was* — without inventing which firm he worked for or what he did on a Tuesday.

## What a shoe jobber did

American footwear moved through a **three-step chain**:

| Role | Did | Did not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Manufacturer** | Made shoes (often upstate / New England factories) | Sell pairs to walk-in customers |
| **Jobber** | Bought bulk lots; kept assortments; sold smaller lots (often on credit) to retailers | Own the factory floor as such |
| **Retailer** | Sold pairs to the wearer | Buy factory-scale shipments alone |

Wikipedia’s merchandising article still defines a historical **jobbing house** as a business that “buys goods and bulk products from importers, other wholesalers, or manufacturers, and then sells to retailers.”

A 1903 essay on “Present Day Jobbing” (James H. Ritter; cited on that Wikipedia page) put the chain in one breath:

> As commonly practiced, the process of distribution is threefold, comprising the activities of the manufacturer, jobber, and retailer. The manufacturer makes the goods from raw material and sells them in large quantities to the jobber, who in turn distributes in smaller lots, and oftentimes on easier terms, to the retailer. … The jobber is an intermediary who assembles various lines of goods, carries a large and assorted stock, and … sells these goods to the retailer in small assorted lots, while the retailer supplies the consumer.

Business historian Alfred D. Chandler Jr. (via open summaries of *The Visible Hand*) notes that by the late 1860s the full-line wholesaler / jobber had become central in marketing **boots and shoes** among other consumer goods — traveling salesmen (“drummers”) carried samples and credit news to country and city storekeepers.

**So:** “Jobber, shoe business” = **wholesale middleman in footwear**, not “cobbler who made shoes by hand” and not “clerk in a shoe store.”

## Where the NYC trade sat

Newspapers called downtown Manhattan’s wholesale footwear cluster the **Duane Street shoe district** (near Hudson / West Broadway) for decades — the distributing end of the trade, not the later Greenwich Village retail strip.

- *New York Times*, 16 Aug 1912: “Momentary quiet prevails in the **Duane Street shoe district**.”
- *New York Times*, 18 Sep 1927: “The New York **shoe distributing district**, which for nearly half a century has centered at **Duane and Hudson Streets**…”

Monroe’s known addresses were **uptown** (131 E 61 St at marriage 1893; 245 Fort Washington Ave at death 1921). Living uptown and working (or dealing) in a downtown wholesale trade was ordinary; we do **not** have a directory line proving his loft address.
