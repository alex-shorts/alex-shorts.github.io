### Bowman Church, “The Old Slide” — by Mrs. A. McCulloch (via fadedgenes)

The Bowman log chapel was the first meeting house to be erected by any sect in Ancaster. It was raised in 1808, but plans had been made for it much earlier because Benjamin Smith recorded in his diary for December, 1800, that he “scoured logs for the new chapel,” meaning that he peeled the bark off the logs. In 1817, it was still the only meeting house in the township, although the Rev. Ralph Leeming, an Anglican, had begun his ministry here a year earlier. The Rev. William Case formed the Ancaster Circuit in 1808, and Bowman Chapel became the base from which the circuit rides operated until the War of 1812 and later.

After the war, the flow of American settlers into Canada dwindled due to the antipathies on both sides; therefore British settlers were encouraged to come, and they preferred Wesleyan Methodist preachers. This caused friction with the American Methodist Episcopal preachers which helped to bring on Henry Ryan’s break away, and his establishment of the Canadian Wesleyans, or Ryanites.

Bowman Chapel was built on the lot line between land donated by Peter Bowman and by Henry Hagle, the latter becoming one of the Ryanite leaders in later years. The Methodist Episcopal services in the Chapel were so disturbed by the Ryanites that it became impossible to continue them, therefore, early one morning in about the year 1830, Peter Bowman and a group of his loyal followers, with pike poles, crow bars, levers, and rollers, moved the log chapel over onto Peter Bowman’s side of the lot line. Squire Hagle promptly sued them of 500 pounds (sterling) damages. Dr. John Rolph of Dundas, a former Ancaster doctor and lawyer, was counsel for the Bowmanites, but the jury awarded 5 pounds to Squire Hagle. All was quiet for a time, but one morning Peter Bowman awoke to find that the chapel had been moved during the night to Henry Hagle’s side of the boundary. Peter at once summoned his friends and slid the chapel back, but wisely, he slid it only to its original location, on the lot line. This ended the tension, but for many long years after, the chapel was known as “The Old Slide,” or “The Sliding Church.”

Ancaster’s Bowman Church was chosen as the site of the first Canadian Conference of the Methodist Episcopal Church, held August 28, 1829. Thirty church leaders met and made some lasting decisions. They decided to create a Book Room (later Ryerson Press); to establish an Upper Canada Academy (later Victoria College); to publish a church newspaper, the Christian Guardian … and they organized Canada’s first Total Abstinence Society. Egerton Ryerson was ordained at that conference…

In 1845, a new, brick chapel replaced The Old Slide, and was built squarely on the lot line. … The new chapel was dedicated December 7, 1845… After the dedication, The Old Slide was moved away… The new brick chapel served its congregation well until April 23, 1881, when Wesley Spohn and Elgin Smith were burning off the dead grass in the cemetery. A sudden gust of wind swept sparks onto the roof of a shed which soon burned, and with it the church… The stewards took a subscription list among the big crowd that gathered, with such good results that a year later, on March 12, 1882, the present Bowman Church was dedicated.

The cornerstone, dated 1845, of the first brick chapel was put into the wall of the present church by the contractor, Edward Smith.

### Rev. Andrew Prindle — chair, leather marriage book, Kitty Murray, burial, 1935 cairn

The Rev. Andrew Prindle (1780-1855) was minister at Bowman Church in 1809, and, after his superannuation, retired to Ancaster. He became so fat in middle life that a special buggy, with extra strong springs, had to be built for him, and a very wide chair was made for him. This unique chair is still preserved in Bowman Church as a prized relic of this popular preacher. He kept a small, leather bound book in which is recorded the marriage of Catharine House in 1843 to a Mr. Murray. Her monument is Kitty Murray Lane, which is named after her. Andrew Prindle and his wife lie buried on the east side of Bowman Church. In 1935, a cairn was built to hold his fallen tombstone, and a memorial service was conducted by the Rev. T. Albert Moore, a grandson of Mr. Moore of Milton who made the reinforced buggy.
