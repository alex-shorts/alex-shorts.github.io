## Catalog note

**The next scene after the English wars.** Haynes (Stonington/manor historian) says the veteran lost the **Roxbury Train Band** captaincy in **1650** (elders remembered boyish pranks and leaving the baby girls), left in anger for **Pequot**, and was **promptly named captain** there. The drummer called the band three times in two years; Denison posted men and gathered families in the church. In **1654** the same man was port inspector, granted **200 acres**, and **fined 24 shillings** for selling strong waters to an Indian — then built a stockaded lean-to at Pequotsepos with Ann, John, and the rest.

## Excerpts

> George Denison’s uneventful career in Roxbury was interrupted in 1650. A new captain was to be elected commander of the Train Band, the militia company in which every Massachusetts male between 16 and 60 must drill regularly. The young men supported him for this post. Their elders, remembering boyish pranks and the inexplicable desertion of the baby daughters, favored a more stable character, and Denison was beaten by a close vote. … In a gust of anger he left for the new settlement in Connecticut at Pequot.

> GEORGE DENISON’s feelings, outraged by the Roxbury election, were sweetly salved at New London where he was promptly named captain of the Train Band. In the frontier community this was no honorary commission. Three times during the next two years the drummer paraded the hilly streets of the riverside town beating out the alarm that called the Train Band to active duty. … He stationed his men, with an ample supply of ammunition, at three strategic outposts and gathered the women and children for safety in the church and two nearby houses. The attacks did not come…

> He was alloted a choice house lot of six acres on what is now Hempstead Street…

> In 1654 the local records contain three curiously contradictory entries about Captain George Denison. He was commissioned Inspector of the Port of New London, a political plum with juicy fees. He was granted 200 acres of land for services to the community. And he was fined 24 shillings for selling strong waters to an Indian.

> Behind the hills flanking the Mystic to the east lies another valley. Through this winds a brook called by the Indians Pequotsepos, the little river of the Pequots. Here was George Denison’s land and here on a rocky knoll overlooking a great meadow with a glimpse of the ocean beyond, he erected a little lean-to and surrounded it with a stout stockade. Here, in 1654, he brought his wife, their two sons, John and George, their daughters, Ann and Borodell, and the two little girls by his first wife, Sarah and Hannah.
